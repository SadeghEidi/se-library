# Slide craft

## The title is the conclusion

The single highest-value habit in deck writing, and the one consultancies drill
hardest. The heading of a slide carries the "so what", not the subject.

- Not "Retention" but "Retention is where the money leaks"
- Not "Q4 results" but "Q4 doubled on the back of one channel"
- Not "Competitive landscape" but "Nobody else serves the merchant we serve"

A topic title makes the audience wait for the speaker to tell them the point. A
conclusion title lets them read it in two seconds and then listen.

## Word budget

- **Title**: under 12 words, one line at 66px on a 1920 canvas.
- **Supporting sentence**: one. If there are two, the second is another slide.
- **A card**: 20 words. It is a label with a clause, not a paragraph.
- **A whole slide**: if it passes about 40 words it has become a document.

The speaker says the rest. A slide the audience has to read is a slide during
which nobody is listening to the person who came to talk.

## Three, not five

Three cards, three metrics, three columns. Four when the content genuinely has
four parts. At five the eye stops comparing and starts scanning, and the
comparison was the reason for the layout.

## Charts

- **One chart, one message**, and the message is the title.
- **Colour only the series that carries the argument.** Everything else grey.
  A chart where every bar is coloured has no point of view.
- **No chartjunk**: no gridlines you do not need, no 3D, no drop shadows on
  data, no legend when a direct label will do. Label the bars, kill the legend.
- **Start bars at zero.** Truncating an axis to make a trend look steeper is a
  thing an audience catches, and it costs the whole deck's credibility.
- Anything needing more chart than a bar, a line or a stacked bar is an
  appendix table, not a slide.

## Tables

No vertical rules, no zebra striping, no boxed cells. A hairline under the
header row and nothing else. Right-align numbers, use tabular figures, and bold
the one row that is the point. Whitespace does the aligning.

## Every number carries its definition and its source

On the slide, in the `.source` line: what the figure counts, which table or
system it came from, and over what dates. That rule does not relax because the
finding is on a slide. A number
without a definition is a number the room has to take on trust, and the first
person to ask "what is in that?" derails the meeting.

## The appendix

Build it. Put the working, the sensitivities, the country splits and the
methodology behind a divider slide. Then say it exists. An appendix is what
lets the main deck be short without the deck feeling thin, and it is what makes
the hard question a slide flip instead of a promise to follow up.

## The last slide

Never "Thank you". Never "Questions?". The last slide is the specific thing
being asked for, in numbers, with the next step and a date under it. It is the
slide that stays on screen through the entire discussion, so it should be the
thing you want them looking at while they decide.
