# Which spine, for which room

Pick one before writing slides. The frameworks below disagree with each other
because they are solving different rooms, and using an investor spine for a
board review (or the reverse) is the most common reason a good deck lands flat.

| Room | Spine | Length | The last slide is |
|---|---|---|---|
| Investor, first meeting | Sequoia / YC outline | 12 to 15 | The raise, the number, the use of funds |
| Sales, a prospect | Andy Raskin strategic narrative | 8 to 12 | The specific next step and its date |
| Partner or BD | Problem we share, what each side brings, the split | 10 to 14 | What you want them to commit to |
| Board or exec review | Pyramid principle, answer first | 8 to 12 + appendix | The decision being asked for |
| Internal product pitch | Situation, complication, question, answer | 6 to 10 | What you will do next week |
| Conference or keynote | Duarte sparkline: what is vs what could be | 20 to 40 | The call to adventure, not a summary |

## Investor: the Sequoia outline

Read from the source, 2026-09-03
(`sequoiacap.com/article/writing-a-business-plan`), which is the guide Airbnb
used. Ten sections, in this order:

1. **Company purpose** — one declarative sentence. Sequoia's own note is that
   this is harder than it looks and that decks default to listing features
   instead of stating a mission.
2. **Problem** — the customer's pain, how it is handled today, and where those
   workarounds fall short.
3. **Solution** — the eureka moment. Why the value is unique, why it endures.
4. **Why now** — why this has not been built already.
5. **Market potential** — the customer and the market. Some of the best
   companies invent their own.
6. **Competition and alternatives** — direct and indirect, with a plan to win.
7. **Business model** — how it thrives.
8. **Team** — the story of the founders.
9. **Financials** — "if you have any, please include", verbatim. Absent
   financials are not a hole to fill with a fabricated forecast.
10. **Vision** — what exists in five years if this goes well.

**Two things it does not have, which most templates add.** There is no separate
product section, product lives inside solution. And there is no ask slide:
Sequoia's list ends on vision. Sam's decks still end on the ask, because a
meeting with no requested decision wastes the room, but know that this is an
addition rather than the canon.

**Why now is the slide most decks skip and the one that most often decides the
meeting.** It is the argument that this is possible today and was not two years
ago.

The whole point of the outline is that an investor is pattern-matching against
hundreds of decks and looking for the sections in the order they expect them.
Do not be clever with the order.

**Guy Kawasaki's 10/20/30** (ten slides, twenty minutes, thirty point type) is
the same shape, compressed, and the type-size rule is the useful half: it is a
word-count limit disguised as a font size.

## Sales: the strategic narrative

Andy Raskin's shape, and it is not a product pitch:

1. Name a big, undeniable **change in the world**. Not your product. The change.
2. Show there will be **winners and losers** because of it.
3. Tease the **promised land**: what life looks like on the right side of it.
4. Introduce your capabilities as the **magic gifts** for getting there.
5. Present **evidence** that you can deliver it: proof, not claims.

The product does not appear until step four. A sales deck that opens with the
product has skipped the reason anyone should care.

Read from the source, 2026-09-03 (Raskin, "The Greatest Sales Deck I've Ever
Seen", the Zuora deck). Three mechanisms in it are the reason it works, and
they are easy to lose when the shape is copied without them:

- **A change, deliberately not a problem.** Raskin's point against the standard
  "open with the problem" advice: asserting that a prospect has a problem puts
  them on the defensive, because they may not have noticed it or may not want to
  admit to it. Naming a shift in the world gets them talking about how it
  affects them instead. Same information, opposite posture.
- **Winners and losers exists to beat loss aversion.** People stay with the
  status quo to avoid a possible loss rather than change for a possible gain, so
  step two has to show both futures: adapting ends well, not adapting ends
  unacceptably. One without the other does not move anyone.
- **The prospect is the hero and you are not.** Raskin's framing is Gandalf, not
  Frodo: capabilities are introduced as gifts that get the customer to the
  promised land, never as a feature list. A technical detail nobody would sit
  through in isolation becomes interesting once it is the reason the old way
  cannot get them there.

**The evidence at step five is ranked.** A success story about a customer who
looks like this prospect beats everything else. A product demo is second, and
only when features are shown as the route to the promised land. Claims about
yourself are not evidence.

One more finding worth knowing: Zuora's deck worked partly because the whole
company told the same story, so prospects arrived already convinced the shift
was real. A narrative used only in the deck is doing a fraction of the work it
could.

## Board and exec: answer first

The consulting shape (Minto's pyramid principle). The first content slide states
the answer and the decision requested. Everything after it is support, arranged
so that each level is a summary of the level below. An executive who reads only
slide two must still have the whole answer.

**The appendix is not a dumping ground, it is the structure.** Anything that is
evidence rather than argument moves behind the divider, and you name it out loud
("the country-by-country working is in the appendix"). This is how a 30-slide
analysis becomes a 9-slide decision.

## Keynote: the sparkline

Nancy Duarte's finding, from analysing speeches that moved people: they
oscillate between **what is** and **what could be**, repeatedly, and end on a
call to a new normal. The gap between the two is what generates the energy. A
talk that is only "what could be" is a fantasy; a talk that is only "what is" is
a status report.

## The test that applies to all of them

Read the action titles alone, in order, with no slide bodies. If they do not
make the argument by themselves, the deck does not have a spine yet and design
will not give it one.
